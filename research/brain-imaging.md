Your brain is always hungry. It is about two percent of your body's weight, burns a fifth of your energy, and stores almost none of it. Every thought, movement and memory depends on blood arriving at exactly the right spot, within a second or two of being needed.

The last step of that delivery happens in vessels far thinner than a hair. They are too small for a hospital scanner to see. Our lab builds ultrasound that can see them.

## The brain feeds itself on demand

When a group of brain cells becomes active, the blood vessels around them widen and more blood flows in. Scientists call this **neurovascular coupling**: nerve cells and blood vessels working as one system.

It is why brain scans work at all. A functional MRI does not see neurons firing. It sees the rush of blood that follows.

It is also where a great deal goes wrong. When the link between neurons and vessels breaks down, brain cells go short of oxygen even though the large arteries look perfectly healthy. This kind of small-vessel trouble shows up in stroke, dementia and chronic pain, often early. Much of it happens in **capillaries**, the tiniest vessels of all, where red blood cells squeeze through in single file and one stuck immune cell can block the road.

## Too small and too deep

To understand any of this, you would want to watch individual capillaries, across the whole brain, in a living subject, without opening the skull. No standard tool can.

- **Microscopes** can see capillaries, but only about a millimetre into the brain, and usually through a window cut in the skull.
- **MRI** sees the whole brain, but capillaries are far below what it can resolve.
- **Ultrasound** is safe, fast, portable and reaches deep. But it is blurry. Anything smaller than its wavelength, roughly the width of a hair, smears together.

> We wanted the reach of ultrasound with the sharpness of a microscope.

## Something we can follow

The answer begins with **microbubbles**: tiny bubbles of gas, smaller than a red blood cell, that hospitals have used for decades to make heart scans clearer. Injected into the bloodstream, they go wherever blood goes, and they light up brightly under ultrasound.

![Individual microbubbles seen with ultrasound. Each bright spot is a single bubble travelling through a blood vessel.](media/research/ulm/microbubbles.mp4)

Each bubble shows up as a blurry blob, much bigger than the bubble itself. But here is the key idea: even if a blob is blurry, you can find its *centre* very precisely. The same insight, applied to light, won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

## Follow them, and a map appears

Think of a long-exposure photograph of a city at night. You cannot see the cars, but their headlights draw every road.

We do the same with microbubbles. We pinpoint each one, follow it from frame to frame, and let the paths pile up.

![Microbubble paths building up in the blood vessels of a heart. About half a second of data is enough for the vessel tree to take shape. Colour shows how fast the blood is moving.](media/research/ulm/tracks-heart.mp4)

Every path tells us three things: where a vessel is, which way the blood in it flows, and how fast. Put millions of paths together and you get an image like the one below, sharp enough to show vessels a hundredth of a millimetre across, deep in the brain and through the intact skull. The method is called **ultrasound localization microscopy**.

![The blood vessels of a mouse brain, mapped with ultrasound localization microscopy. Colour indicates the direction of flow.](media/research/brain-imaging-web.jpg)

## The hardest vessels of all

The map is built by counting: many bubbles pass through a vessel, and the vessel appears. That works well for the highways. Capillaries are the residential streets. There are vast numbers of them, and each one sees a bubble only rarely. Wait as long as you like, and most of them stay dark.

This is where our own work comes in. Instead of counting bubbles, we follow a single one for its whole journey. A bubble that leaves a small artery, squeezes through a capillary and comes out into a vein has told us everything about that capillary: that it exists, how long the crossing took, and whether anything got stuck along the way. We call these bubbles **single capillary reporters**.

![Single capillary reporters. A conventional map misses most capillaries. Following individual bubbles along their entire journey fills them in, shown in green, across the whole mouse brain.](media/research/ulm/scare.mp4)

With this approach we mapped capillary networks deep in the mouse brain, through the skull, and measured how individual capillaries behave: how quickly blood crosses them and how often flow stalls. In models of brain inflammation, we could watch the immune response to injury play out one capillary at a time ([Lee et al., *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2509564123)).

## What we want to find out next

Seeing capillaries was the first step. Now we want to use that view to learn something new about how the brain and its blood supply work together.

- When neurons become active, what happens in the capillaries next to them?
- In disease, which vessels fail first, and how early can we tell?
- Can a portable, inexpensive ultrasound scan one day catch that failure at the bedside, and show whether a treatment is fixing it?

These are the questions our lab at USF is set up to answer.

*The same tools have other uses too, such as [checking the effect of treatments](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.10.19.563176) that act on the brain's vessels, and [mapping how the brain responds](https://doi.org/10.1109/TBME.2024.3352025) when a nerve elsewhere in the body is treated. Much of the work described here was carried out during Dr. Lee's training at Columbia University and Polytechnique Montréal, with the collaborators named on each paper.*
